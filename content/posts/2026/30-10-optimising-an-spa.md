## Introduction

My initial goal for this post was to prove that the web has become unnecessarily slow. We have miraculous hardware - data travels at the speed of light and processors execute billions of instructions a second. Given that a webpage is ultimately just styled text and boxes, it should be lightning fast, right? But instead, the gold standard is a 2.5s Largest Contentful Paint (LCP). I considered this to be silly - surely we can do way better given the hardware we have?

To prove it, I built and optimised a [scoreboard Single Page Application (SPA)](https://thescoreboard.app). Testing against production on my own laptop, I'd done it: an LCP of 0.3s. Everything felt blazingly fast - navigation was instant, and actions felt very responsive.

Then I ran the tests on a simulated slow 4G connection with a slow CPU. More than 2.5 seconds. Even after optimising almost everything I could think of, I still couldn't get below 2s - barely below the 2.5s I thought was laughably slow. How could a snappy, lightweight SPA feel instantaneous to use, yet fail the very metric designed to measure speed?


## The Trap: Goodhart's Law

LCP measures the time between your browser requesting a web page, and the largest element in the viewport rendering. This is an excellent metric for sites where the initial load is important. Anything where above-the-fold information is crucial. Reducing LCP can have a real impact - Walmart found that their revenue increased by 1% for every 100ms reduction in page load time [WPO stats](https://wpostats.com/2015/11/04/walmart-revenue/).

So, naturally LCP became the focus of my optimisation. But, what started as a metric used as a proxy to definitively show if a change improved performance, quickly became *THE* number to reduce, rather than just an indicator of performance. Despite being aware of its shortcomings, it was the easiest concrete indicator of my optimisations, and I put more weight on it than I should've. It took an over-engineered architectural pivot for me to realise that I'd taken it too far. I'd started sacrificing DX without making a noticeable difference to a user's real experience.

I'd fallen victim to Goodhart's law:

<figure class="quote">
  <blockquote>When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.</blockquote>
  <figcaption>- <a href="https://archive.org/details/ImprovingRatingsAuditInTheBritishUniversitySystem">Marilyn Strathern, 1997</a></figcaption>

## LCP Was The Wrong Metric

It wasn't just my obsession with LCP that made it an unsuitable metric. It's important to know the limitations of metrics - I'd argue LCP is often not the best fit for measuring performance of an SPA.

For my scoreboard app, all the pages are behind a login - you can't do anything on the site without logging in. That means to load a page, it needs to authenticate the user before it can load any data required for the page. That means 3 round trips - first fetching `index.html` and the JS bundle, then another to check the authentication state, and finally another for the page-specific data. Let's look at how that affects LCP on devices with a slower internet connection.

Here's our scenario - each request depends on the last, and can't be sent off until the previous one returns:
![Sequential waterfall](/images/optimising-an-spa/sequential-waterfall.svg)

However, if all these requests could be made in parallel, the waterfall looks a lot better - far less impact from a high latency:
![Parallel waterfall](/images/optimising-an-spa/parallel-waterfall.svg)

That's an unfortunate consequence of the architecture I chose. I've tried to reduce the waterfall wherever possible, but there are some limitations of a client-side rendered application that are too hard to work around. But does that mean the app is slow? Not necessarily - if users spend time interacting with the website, performing actions, the initial load is way less important than the speed of subsequent actions. The performance a real user experiences in your website may change entirely depending on *how* they use it.


### The Lab vs. Field Disconnect

A real user's real experience is important. Metrics have limitations, but it's even worse when you only measure lab performance. Your user's experience is difficult to distill into a single synthetic benchmark. And when you are guessing how a user uses the app, and what hardware they may be using, the data you collect is skewed. By using Real User Monitoring (RUM), you can record every interaction actual visitors have with your website. With this, you get a way better insight into how your website is actually experienced by real people.

> [!NOTE]
> This isn't always possible - without a large user-base, I was unable to measure a reliable P75. The best I could do was assume Lighthouse's "throttled" mode would reflect a P75. But the reality is it varies a lot, depending on who uses the website. Maybe most of your users have the latest and greatest hardware and a stable internet connection. Or maybe they're on decade old hardware with an intermittent internet connection. That wildly changes the P75.

So I had a double whammy - I was using the wrong metric, under lab conditions. It meant I missed low hanging fruit to improve the perceived speed of the app. Notably, optimistic rendering on actions. Because this wouldn't move the needle on LCP, I didn't end up implementing this, even though it would have made interactions feel instantaneous.


## Pragmatic Wins: Staying Lean

Whilst LCP is an imperfect metric and not best suited for my project, it did lead to a lot of real optimisations. The biggest one was cutting out bloat and unnecessary libraries. In the JavaScript ecosystem, it's easy to reach for a library to do something for you, but it's not always necessary. Including an entire library for one or two function calls often isn't worth it. It's worth evaluating what you need from the library, and if it's better to just write the functionality yourself. Cutting a library out reduces your bundle size and the attack surface for supply chain attacks.

A fast/lean tech stack also helped me a lot. I hadn't set out to make this extremely optimised, but the stack I was intrigued by happened to be fast. Here's the setup:
- TypeScript + SolidJS front end - SolidJS provides a fine-grain reactivity that outperforms React
- Rust backend - Rust is known for being "blazingly fast", I happen to love the DX using Rust and its speed meant near instant request processing times
- VPS - I hosted everything on a single VPS, meaning the server and database were on the same device. This made database lookups in single digit milliseconds, rather than 2 or 3 digits for a round trip to another server across the world. This also avoided the start-up times of serverless applications, whilst still being affordable (~£5 a month)
- Single-node Postgres - instead of spinning up a multi-region NoSQL cluster because it ["shards automatically and is web scale"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b2F-DItXtZs), I stuck with Postgres on the same $5 VPS as the API. This dropped query latency to single-digit milliseconds
- Cloudflare CDN - despite my measured LCP getting worse when using Cloudflare as a CDN (I'll explain why in my next post), it was still a net win. Cloudflare globally caches the static JS bundle for my web app, cutting down long distance network travel for distant users. Plus it comes with free DDoS protection.

The fast architecture didn't solve the fundamental problem of my slow LCP (multiple round trips), but it definitely helped to make the app feel snappy, and prevented my server/front end being the bottle neck. Instead, latency was the bottleneck.

Finally, I had several ideas for how to improve the LCP, but I stopped before implementing many of them because I realised they would hinder DX or actual user-experienced performance. For example, my big idea was to use my Rust web server to serve the HTML files. By doing this, I could also inject the relevant data for that page directly into the HTML, cutting out the round trips of authentication and fetching page data. This would've helped LCP for sure, but then I realised that LCP is probably not what users care about here. Yes, they want the scoreboard to load quickly, but more importantly they'd want to be able to click between users, view their states, make changes, and record matches quickly over the initial load being fast. So I deemed the added complexity (of what was essentially hand-rolled SSR) was unnecessary with such minimal UX gain for the DX burden. A principle I have been loving recently is [Keep It Simple, Stupid (KISS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KISS_principle), and that's a big reason I stopped before implementing this.

In my next post, I'll be going over what exactly I did to achieve performance improvements, as I believe improving it was still incredibly valuable even if it wasn't as fast as I'd hoped to achieve.


## Was I Too Harsh on the Web?

So, with all that let's answer the question - was I too harsh on the web? In many aspects, yes. I was overconfident and believed the web could easily be made more performant, but I was humbled when it came to trying to optimise my own web app. SPAs especially have a lot going on (e.g. authentication, data loading, etc.), and my app is a fairly simple CRUD app - many web apps are way more complicated, and therefore it's understandable if they're slower. I think a large part of the problem is the metrics we have. We need something better than LCP for measuring the UX of SPAs, especially ones behind an authentication wall.

However, many websites still feel sluggish to me. I admit that they're probably doing a lot more than meets the eye. But performance can often be overlooked or deprioritised when developing a website - I'm guilty of it too. It's easy to reach for a familiar/popular framework when there is a simpler, faster alternative. If a website is static, it doesn't need a framework like React or SolidJS, and can achieve a sub 1s LCP even on throttled devices. And even if there are hard limitations, such as multiple round-trips required to authenticate and fetch data, there are tricks that can make a web app feel fast (optimistic rendering to name one). As developers, we love to over-engineer solutions. It may be fun, but it's usually not the best approach for UX or even DX. KISS!


## If Not LCP, Then What?

So we've established that LCP isn't the best fit for SPAs, where the initial load matters way less than subsequent interactions with the page. But what can we use instead?


### All Metrics Suck

Before we look at possible metrics, I want to mention that all metrics are limited. They measure one specific attribute. But performance isn't that simple - it's about psychology, what the human mind perceives as fast, not how low we can get metric numbers. For example, there are many ways UX and perceived performance/responsiveness can be improved to keep users engaged that likely won't affect metrics, including optimistic updates and loading states that give feedback to users.

Measuring perceived performance is very complicated and less consistent than well-defined metrics. It's something to bear in mind, but almost impossible to measure.


### Alternative Metrics

There are a few metrics that could be used for SPAs, but many are not well defined or standardised. Let's look at a few I found:

| Metric Name | What It Measures | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interaction to Next Paint (INP) | The time between the user clicking an element on the screen and the next render | Fooled by loading spinners - if a loading state is immediately rendered after the user interacts with an element, INP is tiny, even if the action actually takes a long time to complete |
| Soft LCP | This mimics traditional LCP but for soft navigations. It's recorded when a user clicks something, the URL changes, and new DOM elements are rendered | Only in Google Chrome, and is experimental |
| Time to settled | Duration from user interaction until the final data has loaded and the UI has stopped updating | Manual implementation - the browser doesn't know when the page has stopped loading |
| Request duration | Time for requests to complete | Manual implementation, and doesn't necessarily reflect how fast the UI updates |

With an SPA, there's so much variation in how the app functions and what metrics matter. This makes it really difficult to standardise the metrics, and often means you have to do custom performance testing. There are solutions such as OpenTelemetry which allow you to trace actions/requests through your entire stack. It's incredibly useful for debugging performance bottlenecks, but it can be a bit overkill and add bloat. The browser already has built in functions such as `performance.now()` which adds no bloat, but requires a bit of manual work figuring out what to track and how.


## Conclusion

So, where do we go from here?

For me, I think there's one glaring optimisation I can make on my web app - optimistic rendering. While this won't improve LCP, it'll definitely improve UX. Following that, I think RUM is incredibly useful. I've now implemented RUM reporting, and I log all the stats on my server for me to review. If my scoreboard web app ever gets users outside me and my friends, then this will be incredibly helpful. I plan on adding more metrics here, and maybe also setting up automated performance tests for these metrics using Playwright.

A key takeaway for me is that it's important to pick the right metric. That may mean inventing a custom metric that describes a facet of your app that you are keen to optimise, but that's worth it. A personalised metric can go a long way in helping you move in the right direction for UX. Simulated performance is incredibly useful for a reasonably consistent metric, which makes it great for tracking whether a change improved or worsened performance. But it's important to remember that metrics have limitations, and should not be relied on too heavily.

As for the state of the web - I've gained a new respect for the performance of SPAs. I'm now more forgiving if they aren't as snappy as I'd hoped. However, I do still think optimisation should be considered more. Many people do not have as powerful hardware as us developers, and we should consider them more when developing web apps. I believe that whatever website you've made, it's worth the time spent to find how it could be optimised, even if it only improves the experience for people with low powered devices. In my next blog post, I'll outline the approach I took to optimisation and hopefully you'll be able to apply some of that knowledge to your own website!
