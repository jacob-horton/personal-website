## Introduction

My initial goal for this post was to prove that the web has become unnecessarily slow. We have miraculous hardware - data travels at two thirds of the speed of light and processors execute billions of instructions a second. Given that a webpage is ultimately just styled text and boxes, it should be lightning fast, right? But instead, the threshold for a "good" website is a 2.5s Largest Contentful Paint (LCP). I considered this to be silly - surely we can do better given the hardware we have?

To prove it, I built and optimised a [scoreboard Single Page Application (SPA)](https://thescoreboard.app). Testing against production on my own laptop, I'd done it: an LCP of 0.3s. Everything felt blazingly fast - navigation was instant, and actions felt very responsive.

Then I ran the tests on a simulated slow 4G connection with a slow CPU. More than 2.5 seconds. Even after optimising almost everything I could think of, I still couldn't get below 2s - barely below the 2.5s I thought was laughably slow. How could a snappy, lightweight SPA feel instantaneous to use, yet fail the very metric designed to measure speed?


## The trap: Goodhart's law

LCP measures the time from the start of navigation until the largest qualifying content element is rendered. This is an excellent metric for sites where the initial load is important. Anything where above-the-fold information is crucial. Reducing initial load time can have a real impact - Walmart found that their revenue increased by up to 1% for every 100ms reduction in page load time ([WPO stats](https://wpostats.com/2015/11/04/walmart-revenue/)).

So naturally, LCP became the focus of my optimisation. But what started as a metric used as a proxy to definitively show if a change improved performance quickly became *THE* number to reduce, rather than just an indicator of performance. Despite being aware of its shortcomings, it was the easiest concrete indicator of my optimisations, and I put more weight on it than I should've. It took an over-engineered architectural pivot for me to realise that I'd taken it too far. I'd started sacrificing DX without making a noticeable difference to a user's real experience.

I'd fallen victim to Goodhart's law. [Marilyn Strathern's 1997 formulation](https://archive.org/details/ImprovingRatingsAuditInTheBritishUniversitySystem) puts it nicely:

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.


## I was asking too much of LCP

It wasn't just my obsession with LCP that made it an unsuitable metric. It's important to know the limitations of metrics, and I'd argue LCP is not the best fit for measuring overall performance of an SPA. While useful, initial load time is not the be-all and end-all of SPA performance, it's a sliver.

For my scoreboard app, all the pages are behind an authentication wall - you can't do anything on the site without logging in. Before the browser can load any page-specific data, it must first authenticate the user. This creates a chain of round trips, each waiting on the previous one to finish. Let's look at how that affects LCP on devices with a slower internet connection.

Here's our (simplified) scenario - we have three requests. The first asks for the `index.html` and JS bundle. The JS then sends a request to authenticate the user. And finally, once authenticated, it can make the request for the page data:
![Sequential waterfall](/images/optimisation-failures/sequential-waterfall.svg)

However, if all these requests could be made in parallel, the waterfall looks a lot better - far less impact from a high latency:
![Parallel waterfall](/images/optimisation-failures/parallel-waterfall.svg)

That's an unfortunate consequence of the architecture I chose. I've tried to reduce the waterfall wherever possible, but there are some limitations of a client-side rendered application that are too hard to work around. But does that mean the app is slow? Not necessarily - if users spend time interacting with the website, performing actions, the initial load is far less important than the speed of subsequent actions. Load performance and interaction performance are different problems. The performance a real user experiences on your website may change entirely depending on *how* they use it.


### The lab vs. field disconnect

A real user's real experience is important. Metrics are skewed when you only measure lab performance. Your user's experience is difficult to distill into a single synthetic benchmark. And when you are guessing how a user uses the app, and what hardware they may be using, the data you collect is unrepresentative. By using Real User Monitoring (RUM), you can record how actual visitors interact with your website and the performance they experience. With this, you get a way better insight into how your website is actually experienced by real people.

> [!NOTE]
> This isn't always possible - without a large user base, I was unable to measure a reliable P75. I used Lighthouse's "throttled" mode as a consistent test environment. But it wasn't a genuine P75 estimate - the reality is it varies a lot, depending on who uses the website. Maybe most of your users have the latest and greatest hardware and a stable internet connection. Or maybe they're on decade-old hardware with an intermittent internet connection. That wildly changes the P75.

So I had made two mistakes - I was using the wrong metric, *and* only under lab conditions. It meant I missed low-hanging fruit to improve the perceived speed of the app - most notably, optimistic UI on actions. Because this wouldn't move the needle on LCP, I didn't end up implementing it, even though it would have made interactions feel instantaneous.


## Pragmatic wins: staying lean

Whilst LCP is an imperfect metric and not best suited for my project, it did lead to a lot of real optimisations. The biggest one was cutting out bloat and unnecessary libraries. In the JavaScript ecosystem, it's easy to reach for a library to do something for you, but it's not always necessary. Including an entire library for one or two function calls often isn't worth it. It's worth evaluating what you need from the library, and if it's better to just write the functionality yourself. Cutting a dependency out can reduce bundle size and the number of third-party dependencies you have to trust and maintain - potentially helping avoid you falling victim to a supply chain attack.

<!-- TODO: Maybe say a tech stack didn't help - raw computational speed wasn't the issue -->
A fast, lean tech stack also helped me a lot. I hadn't set out to make this extremely optimised, but the stack I was intrigued by happened to be fast. Here's the setup:
- **TypeScript + SolidJS frontend** - SolidJS provides a fine-grained reactivity that was built with performance in mind
- **Rust backend** - Rust is known for being "blazingly fast". I happen to love the DX using Rust and its speed meant backend processing times were negligible in my measurements
- **VPS and single-node Postgres** - I hosted everything on a single VPS. Instead of spinning up a multi-region NoSQL cluster because it ["shards automatically and is web scale"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b2F-DItXtZs), I stuck with Postgres on the same $5 VPS as the API. This dropped query latency to single-digit milliseconds
- **Cloudflare CDN** - despite my measured LCP getting worse when using Cloudflare (as it adds overhead for my particular setup), it was still a net win. Cloudflare globally caches the static JS bundle for my web app, cutting down long-distance network travel for distant users. Plus it comes with free DDoS protection

The lean implementation didn't solve the fundamental network-latency problem, but it definitely helped to make the app feel snappy, and prevented my server or frontend computation speed being the bottleneck. Instead, latency and round-trips were the main bottleneck.

Finally, I had several ideas for how to improve the LCP, but I stopped before implementing many of them because I realised they would hinder DX or actual user-experienced performance. For example, my big idea was to use my Rust web server to serve the HTML files. By doing this, I could also inject the relevant data for that page directly into the HTML, cutting out the round trips of authentication and fetching page data. This would've helped LCP for sure, but then I realised that LCP is probably not what users care about here. Yes, they want the scoreboard to load quickly, but more importantly they'd want to be able to click between users, view their states, make changes, and record matches quickly over the initial load being fast. So I deemed the added complexity (of what was essentially hand-rolled SSR) to be unnecessary with such minimal UX gain for the DX burden. A principle I have been loving recently is [Keep It Simple, Stupid (KISS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KISS_principle), and that's a big reason I stopped before implementing this.

In my next post, I'll be going over what exactly I did to achieve performance improvements, as I believe improving it was still incredibly valuable even if it wasn't as fast as I'd hoped to achieve.


## If not LCP, then what?

So, we've established that LCP doesn't give us the full picture of SPA performance. But it shouldn't be abandoned - initial load is still important, we just need a wider view of performance. So what else can we use?


### The inherent flaw in all metrics
<!-- TODO: No single metric captures the whole experience. Rather than metrics are wrong -->

Before we look at possible metrics, I want to mention that metrics measure one specific attribute. But performance isn't that simple - it's about psychology, what the human mind perceives as fast, not how low we can get metric numbers. For example, there are many ways UX and perceived performance/responsiveness can be improved to keep users engaged that likely won't affect metrics, including optimistic UI and loading states that give feedback to users.

Measuring perceived performance is very complicated and less consistent than well-defined metrics. It's something to bear in mind, but almost impossible to measure.


### Alternative metrics

There are a few metrics that could be used for SPAs, but many are not well defined or standardised. Let's look at a few I found:

| Metric Name | What It Measures | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interaction to Next Paint (INP) | The time between the user clicking an element on the screen and the next render | Doesn't measure end-to-end action completion - if a loading state is immediately rendered after the user interacts with an element, INP is tiny, even if the action actually takes a long time to complete |
| [Soft LCP](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/soft-navigations) | This mimics traditional LCP but for soft navigations. It's recorded when a user clicks something, the URL changes, and new DOM elements are rendered | This is one to keep an eye on. It's currently only in Google Chrome, and was released very recently (Chrome 151, July 2026) |
| Time to settled | Duration from user interaction until the final data has loaded and the UI has stopped updating. E.g. the time between a user clicking a save button, and the request completing and updating the UI to reflect that | Manual implementation - the browser doesn't know when the page has stopped loading |
| Request duration | Time for requests to complete | Manual implementation, and doesn't necessarily reflect how fast the UI updates |

With an SPA, there's so much variation in how the app functions and what metrics matter. This makes it really difficult to standardise the metrics, and often means you have to do custom performance testing. There are solutions such as OpenTelemetry which allow you to trace actions/requests through your entire stack. It's incredibly useful for debugging performance bottlenecks, but it can be a bit overkill and add bloat. The browser already has built-in functions such as `performance.now()` which adds no bloat, but requires a bit of manual work figuring out what to track and how. You should pick the metrics that matter to your app first, then you can decide the best route to measure them.


## Was I too harsh on the web?

So, with all that let's answer the question - was I too harsh on the web? In many aspects, yes. Seeing how difficult it is to even *measure* SPA performance correctly cured me of my hubris. I was overconfident and believed the web could easily be made more performant, but I was humbled when it came to trying to optimise my own web app. SPAs especially have a lot going on (e.g. authentication, data loading) - many web apps are way more complicated than my simple CRUD app too. When measuring performance, we just need to be intentional with the metrics we pick, ensuring they're the right fit for the specific app being developed.

<!-- TODO: stronger statement would be “If a site is genuinely static, a client-side application framework may add complexity and bytes without providing much benefit.” -->

However, many websites still feel sluggish to me. I admit that they're probably doing a lot more than meets the eye. But performance can often be overlooked or deprioritised when developing a website - I'm guilty of it too. It's easy to reach for a familiar/popular framework when there is a simpler, faster alternative. If a website is static, it doesn't need a framework like React or SolidJS - by stripping out bloat and using a sensible tech stack, you can achieve a sub-second LCP even on throttled devices. And even if there are hard limitations, such as multiple round-trips required to authenticate and fetch data, there are tricks that can make a web app feel fast (optimistic UI to name one). As developers, we love to over-engineer solutions. It may be fun, but it's usually not the best approach for UX or even DX.


## Conclusion

So, where do we go from here?

I've learnt a lot through this optimisation project. Going forwards, I'm going to be far more cautious when optimising - making sure to optimise an experience, not a number. And that comes from measuring what actual users do - getting RUM, and using a suite of metrics that actually represent how someone would use the app. Each optimisation can come at a cost of DX or even worse UX, so it's important to weigh up the impact it has on your actual users with the architectural cost. It's not worth trading architectural simplicity for a metric nobody notices.
